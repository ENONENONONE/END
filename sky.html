Test 1The five starless figures and the Moon

Of 299 figures exactly five hold no star. Taking each figure as one degree of the Sun's path, in list order, the Moon's age on each of those five days was computed for every year from 1301 to 1500.

1427 repeats 1408 to within 2°, as it must — the two are one nineteen-year cycle apart. Both are the third year of that cycle, one of its long years.

Where it fails

"No star means no Moon" fits Gemini 13 and Cancer 10. The other three fall near first quarter and full Moon. One rule does not yet cover all five.

Test 2f103r's nineteen stars, seven painted red

The last page of the starred text carries nineteen stars, seven of them painted red, at positions 1, 4, 6, 9, 12, 14, 17 from the top. The nineteen-year cycle has seven long years of thirteen months, at years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, 19.

Read from the foot upward — the direction the book is read — six of the seven fall on long years as the page stands, and all seven do on one placement. The page image was checked against the transcribers' notes star by star, and f108r's five dark stars check too.

The join

27 × 257 = 6,939 days, against 6,939.75 days in nineteen Julian years and 6,939.69 in 235 lunar months. The field's modulus and the Metonic cycle agree to within a day, so two dates one cycle apart share a field cell. This is why 1408 and 1427 give the same reading, and why 1413-11-08 and 1432-11-07 both land on cell 153.

f68r3The Moon in the Pleiades

The third star chart draws the Moon as a full round face beside a cluster of seven stars — the other two charts draw a crescent. In the 1400s a full Moon could stand in the Pleiades only in early November, Julian, and only in the years the Moon's path reached the cluster.
